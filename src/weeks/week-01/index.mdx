## Research

I started with loading as a topic rather than with sketches, because the assignment's split between determinate and indeterminate is not a style choice. It is a claim about what the system actually knows.

### The wait decides the type

Nielsen's three thresholds still set the terms. Roughly 0.1 seconds reads as direct causation, 1 second is noticed without breaking your train of thought, and 10 seconds is the edge of abandonment.

![What to show, by how long the wait lasts](./images/response-time-thresholds.svg)

Nielsen's guidance on indicators is that a true percent-done display is overkill for waits between about 2 and 10 seconds, and becomes necessary past 10. Below a second, showing anything at all can make the interface feel slower, because you have drawn attention to a wait the user had not noticed.

This gives me a rule for the assignment. The determinate half of each set belongs to long operations where the system can count. The indeterminate half belongs to short or unmeasurable ones. They are not two styles of the same object.

### What the two words actually commit to

Material's definition is useful because it is strict. Indeterminate means the process is happening but the wait is unknown. Determinate means the indicator must accurately represent what it measures. The interesting rule is the transition: as progress becomes knowable, an indicator should move from indeterminate to determinate, not stay vague.

Two details worth stealing:

- Linear indicators run leading edge to trailing edge, circular ones start at the top and go clockwise. Breaking this reads as a bug, not as a style.
- Material adds a small stop indicator at the end of a determinate track when contrast against the surface falls below 3 to 1. A quiet reminder that a low-contrast tone still has to clear an accessibility floor.

### Waiting is felt, not measured

This is the part I want the tones to work on.

Harrison, Yeo and Hudson tested visual augmentations on progress bars. Ribbing that moves backwards while decelerating cut perceived duration by about 11 percent, with no change to actual duration.

Ziat and colleagues went further in a 2022 study, holding speed and distance constant while varying how progress was divided. More steps, each smaller, read as faster and compressed perceived time. Fewer, larger steps dilated it. Their conclusion is that perceived time depends on the nature of the stimulus, not on the speed or distance of the motion.

That finding is directly usable. Step granularity is a tone control, not a technical detail. A calm tone can afford coarse steps. An urgent tone probably wants fine ones.

Skeleton screens are the other well-known move. A skeleton describes the shape of what is arriving, so the wait becomes a preview instead of a blank. A spinner does the opposite: it puts a focal point on the delay itself. The often-quoted claim that skeletons feel 30 percent faster traces back to blog posts rather than a controlled study, so I am treating the direction as credible and the number as unsupported.

### Function includes being usable

Since the brief insists the designs perform their function, accessibility is part of the grade, not a nicety.

A determinate indicator takes `role="progressbar"` with a live `aria-valuenow`. An indeterminate one uses the same role but omits `aria-valuenow`, which is how "unknown" is expressed to a screen reader. The moving graphic itself should be hidden from assistive tech, with the status carried in a text label such as "uploading, 50 percent". Under `prefers-reduced-motion` the looping animation has to park in a sensible resting state rather than simply vanish.

### Pattern survey

Before designing anything, I put the existing vocabulary on one shared clock so the same real duration could be compared across patterns. The duration buttons sit on Nielsen's thresholds, and the step control tests the Ziat finding directly.

<Demo height="760px" />

What I notice running it at 10 seconds: the numeric readout is the most precise and the least bearable, because there is nothing to watch between updates. The segmented bar at 20 steps feels quicker than the smooth bar over the identical duration, which is the effect the study predicts. The spinner is the only one that tells you nothing except that you are waiting.

### What I am taking into the designs

- The determinate and indeterminate halves of a set should share a tone, not a shape. Shrinking a progress bar into a circle is not the same indicator.
- Step granularity, easing, and direction are where tone lives. They are also measurable, so critique can argue with them.
- Each set needs a named context. An indicator has no tone without something to be the tone of.
- Anything under a second should not get an indicator at all, so all six designs are aimed at waits above that.
